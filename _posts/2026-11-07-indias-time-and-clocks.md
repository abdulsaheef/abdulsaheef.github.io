An examination of India's historical timekeeping systems, the adoption of Indian Standard Time, and a proposed three-zone framework based on longitude. This report brings together the historical development of regional clocks, railway and telegraph standardisation, post-independence policy, and a possible future arrangement that balances national coordination with local daylight.

## Executive Summary

India currently follows one official time zone: **Indian Standard Time (IST), UTC+05:30**, based on the standard meridian of **82°30′ E**, near [Mirzapur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mirzapur) in Uttar Pradesh.

India's east–west geographical span creates a substantial difference between local solar time in its western and eastern regions. A single national clock simplifies administration, transport, communication and national coordination, but it does not align equally well with daylight across the country.

India did not always operate under one uniform clock. During British rule, regional standards such as [[Bombay Time]], [[Calcutta Time]] and [[Madras Time]] coexisted. Madras time became particularly important to railway and telegraph operations. The movement towards a common standard culminated in the adoption and implementation of IST around 1905–1906. Calcutta retained a separate time until 1948, while Bombay's municipal authority adopted IST in 1950.

The proposal developed in this discussion retains IST as the national reference while introducing three regional civil-time standards:

| Proposed zone | Offset from IST | UTC offset | Reference meridian |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Bombay Time (BT) | IST − 00:45 | UTC+04:45 | 71°15′ E |
| Madras Time (MT) | IST − 00:15 | UTC+05:15 | 78°45′ E |
| Calcutta Time (CT) | IST + 00:30 | UTC+06:00 | 90°00′ E |

The proposed system uses longitude to establish reference meridians and approximate zone boundaries. It keeps IST for national coordination and uses regional time for local daily life.

> [!NOTE] Central principle
> India could retain one national time reference while allowing regional civil time to reflect local geography and daylight more closely.

## 1. Why does the United States have several time zones while India has one?

The discussion began by comparing the United States, which spans several time zones, with India, which officially uses one.

India's standard time is **UTC+05:30**, based on **82°30′ east longitude**. The country's territory extends from Gujarat in the west to Arunachal Pradesh in the east. Since the Earth rotates approximately 15 degrees per hour, every degree of longitude corresponds to about four minutes of mean solar time.

This creates a substantial difference between local solar time at India's western and eastern extremes—roughly two hours.

The practical consequences include:

- In the Northeast, sunrise and sunset occur earlier by the clock than in western India.
- In western India, the same IST-based working day begins and ends at a different point in the local daylight cycle.
- A single time standard simplifies national coordination but does not closely follow local solar time everywhere.

The comparison raises a broader question: should geographical scale determine the number of time zones, or can administrative and political considerations justify a single national standard?

## 2. Before a single Indian standard: local time and traditional timekeeping

Before modern national time standardisation, time was commonly understood and regulated locally. Local solar time, observatory time, town clocks and community practices could differ from place to place.

India also had older systems of time measurement, including traditional divisions of the day and water-clock practices in some settings. These practices served different social, religious, astronomical and administrative purposes and should not be confused with modern legal time zones.

During the nineteenth century, railways, telegraph networks, ports and expanding cities made differences between local clocks an increasingly practical problem. Railway timetables and telegraph offices needed common references, while travellers moving between towns could encounter different local times.

## 3. The colonial period: Bombay, Calcutta and Madras times

During the nineteenth century, several time standards operated in British India.

| Historical time | Historical role |
| --- | --- |
| Bombay Time | Used in Bombay and for important local and official purposes; associated with the western presidency and commercial city |
| Calcutta Time | Used in Calcutta, the colonial capital until 1911–12, and for important administrative purposes |
| Madras Time | Based on the Madras Observatory; increasingly used by railways and telegraph systems as a practical common reference |

> [!WARNING] Historical versus proposed offsets
> The historical offsets of Bombay, Calcutta and Madras times were not the same as the proposed offsets in this report. The names are reused for a new model; the old colonial clocks are not being restored.

### Railway Time and the spread of Madras Time

As railway lines expanded, separate local clocks created timetable confusion. During the late nineteenth century, Madras mean time became widely used as a railway reference and was often called **Railway Time** or **Indian Standard Railway Time**.

It was not perfectly universal. Different railway systems and towns could retain local practices, and timetables sometimes had to account for local differences. The coexistence of railway time, telegraph time, court time, bazaar time and local town time was particularly visible in places such as Darjeeling.

Madras time therefore played a major role in India's practical time standardisation before modern IST.

## 4. The push for a single standard: 1899 to 1906

### R. D. Oldham's proposal

In 1899, **R. D. Oldham**, a Geological Survey of India geologist, presented a paper titled *On Time in India* to the Asiatic Society of Bengal.

Oldham argued that a country connected by railways and telegraphs should not continue to rely on a confusing patchwork of local times. He noted that Madras time was already widely used by railways and telegraphs, but local differences continued to complicate everyday and official coordination.

He also identified a political and cultural obstacle: cities such as Bombay and Calcutta were reluctant to accept a standard associated with another presidency, especially Madras. The choice of a national standard was therefore not merely a mathematical question. It involved civic identity, local pride and political authority.

### The Greenwich-based standard

The eventual standard was set at **five hours and thirty minutes ahead of Greenwich Mean Time**, corresponding to the meridian **82°30′ E**. This became [[Indian Standard Time]].

Standardisation was driven by the need for a common reference across railways, telegraphs and government administration. The British colonial state and its departments were central to implementation.

### 1905 and 1906: an important date distinction

Historical sources describe the transition with slightly different dates and stages:

- Some accounts identify **1 July 1905** as the date IST came into effect or was adopted for key official and railway purposes.
- Other railway and time-zone records identify **1 January 1906** as the date the railways and telegraphs switched to UTC+05:30.

These dates should not be flattened into one falsely precise event. They reflect a staged process of proposal, official adoption and operational implementation across institutions.

## 5. Resistance and the “Battle of Clocks”

The introduction of a standard time did not immediately eliminate local times.

### Calcutta

Calcutta had been the capital of British India and retained a strong civic and administrative attachment to Calcutta Time. It continued to use its own time for some official and civic purposes after the wider adoption of IST.

Calcutta's separate time was discontinued in **1948**, after independence.

### Bombay

Bombay's resistance was particularly persistent. The city had a strong civic identity and a history of opposition to externally imposed time changes.

An earlier attempt in 1881 to impose Madras time for municipal and official purposes provoked public opposition. Clocks and public time signals did not all change together; some prominent clocks and time signals continued to display Bombay Time.

The later movement towards IST also became controversial. The Bombay Municipal Corporation held repeated votes in 1927, 1928 and 1929, and the issue became known as the **“Battle of Clocks.”** By the 1930s, many official institutions in Bombay were generally using IST, while the municipal corporation remained a notable holdout.

The Bombay Municipal Corporation finally adopted IST in **1950**.

> [!INFO] Why this history matters
> Standard time is not only a technical instrument. It can become a public issue tied to identity, local autonomy, working routines and trust in government authority.

## 6. Independence and the decision to retain one IST

India became independent in 1947. The new state retained the single national standard of IST rather than restoring the older regional time systems.

The practical advantages were significant:

- A single reference for national administration.
- Common railway and transport timetables.
- Easier communication between states and central institutions.
- A common framework for broadcasting, public announcements and national coordination.
- Continuity with the time standard already used by much of the country.

The final transition away from older city times continued after independence: Calcutta ended its separate time in 1948, and Bombay's municipal authority followed in 1950.

The post-independence model consolidated a standardisation process that had begun under colonial rule. The fact that the system originated in colonial administration does not by itself settle whether it is the best arrangement today; that is a separate policy question.

## 7. India's present arrangement

India currently uses one official time zone:

- **Name:** Indian Standard Time (IST)
- **Offset:** UTC+05:30
- **Reference meridian:** 82°30′ E
- **Reference location:** Near Mirzapur, Uttar Pradesh

There is no officially adopted system of separate regional civil time zones across India. The single standard remains useful for national coordination, but the east–west daylight difference remains.

The central trade-off is between the simplicity of one national clock and the closer relationship between local clock time and daylight that regional time standards could provide.

## 8. The policy question: one time zone or regional time?

### Arguments for retaining one national time

- One time simplifies national transport, communications and administration.
- National institutions can coordinate without routine time conversions.
- Businesses operating across states have one common reference.
- A single standard avoids the complexity of drawing and administering internal time-zone boundaries.

### Arguments for considering regional time

- India's longitudinal span creates a meaningful solar-time difference.
- In eastern regions, daylight begins relatively early by IST, while the national clock can make the morning feel late.
- Regional time could align ordinary work and school schedules more closely with local daylight.
- Other large countries demonstrate that multiple time zones can coexist with national coordination.

### The middle path: UTC → IST → regional time

Rather than abandoning IST, how to keeps it as the national reference while introducing regional clocks for local use.

IST would remain the common national reference, while regional time would be a local offset from IST. This preserves a single national coordination standard without requiring every region to use the same wall-clock time.

## 9. How does the three-zone framework look like?

This regional standards reuse the historical city-time names but introduce new offsets.

| Proposed regional standard | Offset from IST | Equivalent UTC offset | Reference meridian |
| -------------------------- | --------------- | --------------------- | ------------------ |
| Bombay Time (BT)           | IST − 00:45     | UTC+04:45             | 71°15′ E           |
| Madras Time (MT)           | IST − 00:15     | UTC+05:15             | 78°45′ E           |
| Calcutta Time (CT)         | IST + 00:30     | UTC+06:00             | 90°00′ E           |

### Why these meridians?

Mean solar time advances by approximately four minutes for every degree eastward longitude.

- 71°15′ E corresponds to UTC+04:45.
- 78°45′ E corresponds to UTC+05:15.
- 90°00′ E corresponds to UTC+06:00.
- IST remains UTC+05:30, corresponding to 82°30′ E.

The regional standards are therefore defined as differences from IST, not as three independent national standards.

## 10. Proposed zone boundaries

The stated preference was that boundaries should follow longitude meridians, even if they divide existing states. Administrative or district boundaries may be considered as practical refinements, but solar geography should remain the guiding principle.

If the boundary between adjacent zones is placed halfway between their reference meridians, the approximate dividing meridians are:

- **75°00′ E** between Bombay Time (71°15′ E) and Madras Time (78°45′ E).
- **84°22′30″ E** between Madras Time (78°45′ E) and Calcutta Time (90°00′ E).

These are mathematical midpoint proposals, not surveyed or legally defined boundaries. A real boundary would need detailed mapping and decisions about districts, transport links, population, administration and local preferences.

> [!NOTE] Boundary principle
> Longitude is the primary geographical basis. State borders should not automatically override the meridian-based logic, although practical administrative adjustments may be considered.

## 11. Pinpointing the reference locations

The discussion emphasised that each reference meridian should be connected to an identifiable place on the map, rather than merely naming a city.

### Madras Time: 78°45′ E

A geographic lookup identified **Ellakkudi village**, in Tiruchirappalli district, Tamil Nadu, at approximately **10.806° N, 78.7503° E**. The target meridian 78°45′ E is 78.7500° E, so Ellakkudi is extremely close to the proposed reference meridian.

**Devakottai** was also identified in a place-coordinate listing at 78°45′ E. Ellakkudi is the more precise candidate based on the decimal coordinate retrieved in the discussion.

The term “Madras Time” here refers to the proposed regional zone, not a requirement that the meridian pass through Chennai city.

### Other reference meridians

The reference meridians for Bombay Time and Calcutta Time were calculated, but a final named reference location was not yet established:

- Bombay Time: **71°15′ E**
- Calcutta Time: **90°00′ E**

Finding suitable named locations and confirming their coordinates remains open work.

## 12. Fixed office hours

The common local office schedule is **8:00 AM to 5:00 PM** in all three zones, throughout the year.

| Zone | Local office hours | Equivalent IST |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bombay Time | 8:00 AM–5:00 PM BT | 8:45 AM–5:45 PM IST |
| Madras Time | 8:00 AM–5:00 PM MT | 8:15 AM–5:15 PM IST |
| Calcutta Time | 8:00 AM–5:00 PM CT | 7:30 AM–4:30 PM IST |

This proposal uses fixed hours with no seasonal changes and no daylight-saving time.

This schedule is a proposed common local convention. It does not mean every location in a zone experiences identical sunrise or sunset, nor does it automatically optimise daylight in every season.

## 13. How national and regional time would work together

Under the proposed model, IST would continue to be used for national coordination, while the relevant regional time would be used for local daily life.

### Possible national uses of IST

- Parliament and central-government coordination.
- Supreme Court and national institutions.
- National examinations and common announcements.
- National transport and inter-regional scheduling.
- Financial-market schedules and national reporting.
- National broadcasting and emergency communications.

Regional time would be used for local clocks, offices, schools, businesses and ordinary daily schedules.

### Example: converting a national event

A national event announced for **10:00 AM IST** would be shown locally as:

| Region | Local time |
| --- | --- |
| Bombay Time | 9:15 AM |
| Madras Time | 9:45 AM |
| Calcutta Time | 10:30 AM |

The proposed communication convention is to publish IST as the national reference and provide regional equivalents where needed.

## 14. Technical limits and implementation questions

A time-zone proposal needs to distinguish clock time from actual solar conditions.

- A reference meridian defines mean solar time at that longitude. Apparent solar noon does not occur at exactly 12:00 every day because of the equation of time.
- Locations away from a zone's reference meridian will experience a difference between their local mean solar time and the zone clock.
- A wide zone cannot provide perfect solar alignment to every settlement.
- Fixed office hours do not remove seasonal variation in daylight.
- Boundaries based only on longitude may split districts, towns and transport networks. Administrative adjustments could improve usability but would depart from perfectly straight longitude-based borders.
- A real proposal would require legal, administrative, economic, transport, public-service and public-consultation analysis.

> [!WARNING] Policy limitation
> The calculations establish a conceptual time framework. They do not establish that the proposed boundaries, office hours or offsets are optimal. Those questions require geographic modelling, impact assessment and public consultation.

## 15. Decisions established in the discussion

The following are the user's stated design choices, not claims about current government policy:

1. Keep IST as India's national reference standard.
2. Keep IST at UTC+05:30.
3. Express regional standards as offsets from IST.
4. Use three proposed regional standards: Bombay, Madras and Calcutta Time.
5. Use the revised offsets: IST−00:45, IST−00:15 and IST+00:30.
6. Keep all regional clocks fixed throughout the year.
7. Do not introduce daylight-saving or seasonal clock changes.
8. Prefer longitude-based zone boundaries, even where they cross state borders.
9. Use 8:00 AM–5:00 PM as the standard local office schedule in all three zones.
10. Retain IST for national coordination and regional time for local daily life.

## 16. Open questions and next research

- Identify and verify named reference locations close to 71°15′ E and 90°00′ E.
- Map the proposed boundaries across India and assess the resulting state and district divisions.
- Decide how to handle settlements and transport corridors near a boundary.
- Examine the impact on schools, government offices, courts, markets, railways, aviation and broadcasting.
- Compare the proposed three-zone system with a two-zone model or a single IST system with locally adjusted working hours.
- Assess legal and constitutional mechanisms, transition costs and public acceptance.
- Evaluate whether the fixed 8 AM–5 PM schedule works adequately across latitude, longitude and seasonal daylight differences.

## Conclusion

India's time system evolved from local and regional clocks, through railway and telegraph standardisation under British rule, to a single national IST that was consolidated after independence. The history of Bombay and Calcutta shows that time standardisation was never purely technical: it involved administration, civic identity, public resistance and the practical demands of a connected country.

The proposal developed in this conversation seeks a middle path. It retains IST as the national reference while allowing three fixed regional clocks:

- **Bombay Time:** IST − 00:45.
- **Madras Time:** IST − 00:15.
- **Calcutta Time:** IST + 00:30.

Its guiding idea is to reconcile national unity in timekeeping with India's geographic reality and local daylight patterns. It remains a proposal for further study, not an adopted policy.
